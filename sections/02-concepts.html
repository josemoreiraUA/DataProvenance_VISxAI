Provenance semirings and semimodules

Concepts

There are several forms of data provenance that capture complementary aspects of how data is produced and consumed at different levels of granularity. This work focuses on tuple-level provenance, the most fine-grained form of provenance, which identifies the source records that contributed to each output tuple and describes how and why they contributed to its creation. Such fine-grained provenance is particularly valuable for Explainable AI, as it enables users to trace the origin of the data used to train or execute a model, thereby improving the transparency, explainability, and trustworthiness of its predictions.

A provenance semiring is an algebraic structure $( \mathbb{K}, +, \cdot \, , 0, 1)$, where $\mathbb{K}$ denotes a set of provenance tokens, $+$ is a commutative and associative operator with identity \( 0 \), and $\cdot$ is a commutative and associative operator with identity \( 1 \) distributive over $+$ [1]. A provenance token is a unique identifier that represents a source tuple in the input dataset. The semiring addition operator ($+$) represents alternative derivations of a tuple, while the multiplication operator ($\cdot$) represents joint participation of tuples in the same derivation. For instance, when two datasets are joined, the provenance of each resulting tuple is expressed as the conjunction of the provenance annotations of the contributing tuples, such as $t_1 \cdot r_2$, where $t_1$ and $r_2$ are provenance tokens. When a column is projected, for instance to obtain its unique values, the provenance of each resulting tuple is expressed as the sum of the provenance annotations of the contributing tuples, such as $t_1 + t_2$, indicating that the tuple can be derived from multiple alternative inputs. If the contributing tuples are themselves derived from other datasets, their annotations may be provenance polynomials rather than individual tokens.

These algebraic annotations can be combined and propagated through the query evaluation process according to the semantics of the relational algebra Selection, Projection, Join and Union operators, while preserving the semantics of the original query. Thus, the provenance of each output tuple can be expressed as a provenance polynomial that compactly encodes all possible derivations leading to that result.

Aggregation queries require a richer provenance model because the result depends not only on which tuples contributed to the computation, but also on the values they contributed. To address this limitation, provenance semirings are extended by introducing a semimodule structure defined over a semiring and a monoid ($K \otimes M$), where $K$ is a commutative semiring and $M$ is a commutative monoid representing the aggregation domain. For instance, the SUM aggregation is defined over the commutative monoid $(R,+,0)$, where R is the set of real numbers, + is the addition operation and 0 is its identity element [2]. This extension preserves both the computed value and the contribution of every participating tuple, enabling understanding not only which records contributed to the derivation of a data item but also how strongly they influenced aggregated features.

Methods

The theoretical formalisms proposed in [3, 2] have been implemented in provenance frameworks like GProM [4], ProvSQL [5] and DataProv [6], primarily targeting database systems and query processing. They follow similar underlying principles, but their notation and terminology may differ slightly. Complementarily, distributed query engines allow data aggregation across heterogeneous sources, including relational and NoSQL databases, parquet files, excel documents, APIs among other. Together, they can provide a baseline for end-to-end explainability by tracing data contributions across processing pipelines in data analytics and machine learning tasks.

EXAMPLE: Taking DataProv as a structural reference, the system annotates a declarative query and constructs an Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) that represents its logical structure. The AST nodes represent relational operations and the edges data-flow dependencies. The leaf nodes correspond to the underlying data sources. The AST is traversed bottom-up and the provenance annotator maps relational operations to their corresponding algebraic semantics: joins are associated with semiring multiplication, projection and union operations with semiring addition, and aggregation with the appropriate semimodule operations and scalar transformations. The provenance expressions represented as conjunctions, disjunctions and aggregations are then injected into the corresponding AST nodes. They are displayed in the 'Annotated Abstract Syntax Tree' within light blue boxes. Finally, the annotator performs an inverse translation and translates the annotated AST back into a declarative query statement that carries the provenance annotations. The result of a provenance-enabled query can be persisted, together with its provenance information, either in a database or as a dataset. This materialized result can subsequently be treated as a new data source and used recursively in further queries, enabling provenance to be propagated across multiple stages of a data-processing pipeline.
