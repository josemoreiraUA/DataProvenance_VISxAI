Provenance semirings and semimodules

Concepts

There are several forms of data provenance that capture complementary aspects of how data is produced and consumed at different levels of granularity. This work focuses on tuple-level provenance, the most fine-grained form of provenance, which identifies the source records that contributed to each output tuple and describes how and why they contributed to its creation. Such fine-grained provenance is particularly valuable for Explainable AI, as it enables users to trace the origin of the data used to train or execute a model, thereby improving the transparency, explainability, and trustworthiness of its predictions.

A provenance semiring is an algebraic structure $( \mathbb{K}, +, \cdot \, , 0, 1)$, where $\mathbb{K}$ denotes a set of provenance tokens, $+$ is a commutative and associative operator with identity \( 0 \), and $\cdot$ is a commutative and associative operator with identity \( 1 \) distributive over $+$ [REFS]. A provenance token is a unique identifier that represents a source tuple in the input dataset. The semiring addition operator ($+$) represents alternative derivations of a tuple, while the multiplication operator ($\cdot$) represents joint participation of tuples in the same derivation. For instance, when two datasets are joined, the provenance of each resulting tuple is expressed as the conjunction of the provenance annotations of the contributing tuples, such as $t_1 \cdot r_2$, where $t_1$ and $r_2$ are provenance tokens. When a column is projected, for instance to obtain its unique values, the provenance of each resulting tuple is expressed as the sum of the provenance annotations of the contributing tuples, such as $t_1 + t_2$, indicating that the tuple can be derived from multiple alternative inputs. If the contributing tuples are themselves derived from other datasets, their annotations may be provenance polynomials rather than individual tokens.

These algebraic annotations can be combined and propagated through the query evaluation process according to the semantics of the relational algebra Selection, Projection, Join and Union operators, while preserving the semantics of the original query. Thus, the provenance of each output tuple can be expressed as a provenance polynomial that compactly encodes all possible derivations leading to that result.

Aggregation queries require a richer provenance model because the result depends not only on which tuples contributed to the computation, but also on the values they contributed. To address this limitation, provenance semirings are extended by introducing a semimodule structure defined over a semiring and a monoid ($K \otimes M$), where $K$ is a commutative semiring and $M$ is a commutative monoid representing the aggregation domain. For instance, the SUM aggregation is defined over the commutative monoid $(R,+,0)$, where R is the set of real numbers, + is the addition operation and 0 is its identity element [REF]. This extension preserves both the computed value and the contribution of every participating tuple, enabling understanding not only which records contributed to the derivation of a data item but also how strongly they influenced aggregated features.

Methods

The theoretical formalisms proposed in [REF1, REF2] have been implemented in provenance frameworks like Perm [REF], GProM [REF], ProvSQL [REF] and DataProv [REF], primarily targeting database systems and query processing. They follow the same underlying principles, but their notation and terminology may differ slightly. These provenance representations also provide a foundation for end-to-end explainability by enabling data contributions to be traced from their sources through processing pipelines to analytical and machine learning outcomes.

Distributed query engines enable data to be queried and combined across heterogeneous sources without requiring all data to be centralized [REFS]. In a provenance context, they can preserve lineage across these distributed operations, enabling end-to-end tracing from original data sources to the final model inputs.

Taking DataProv as a structural reference model, the system annotates a declarative query statement—such as SELECT ...—by first compiling its syntax tokens into a hierarchical Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) that maps out the underlying relational algebra execution plan. In accordance with the foundational theories of provenance semirings and semi-modules, the engine systematically translates these database operators into mathematical structures, mapping projections and selections to semiring multiplications, union combinations to semiring additions, and aggregate processing workflows to tensor scale modifications. Once these algebraic polynomial variables are injected directly into the corresponding AST expression nodes, the compiler performs an inverse translation rewrite to reconstruct a fully valid, synthesized target SQL statement equipped with active execution provenance instrumentation layers.
