Summary

Traditional XAI methods, such as SHAP and LIME, primarily explain model outputs in terms of the features provided to the model, while the upstream data preparation and transformation processes remain outside the scope of the explanation. In contrast, data provenance provides a formal and deterministic representation of the derivation of data, enabling the dependencies between source records, intermediate results and final inputs to a model to be explicitly traced. This supports the analysis and debugging of data processing pipelines, the identification of source-level causes and the verification of how data transformations contribute to downstream results. When combined with model-level explanation techniques, provenance can complement feature-level explanations by tracing the origin and derivation of the feature values used by the model. For example, when a feature is identified as influential, provenance can be used to determine how its value was derived and whether transformations such as missing-value imputation, filtering, aggregation, or integration of heterogeneous sources contributed to the observed result. This combination provides an end-to-end perspective in which model-level explanations can be related to the underlying data and processing steps, supporting the systematic analysis of the factors that contribute to automated decisions.

Data provenance algebraic annotations precisely capture data dependencies, but they become difficult to interpret when dealing with large datasets or complex data-processing pipelines. Interactive graphical visualizations provide a complementary means of exploring provenance by making data derivation paths and their underlying dependencies more accessible to users. However, relatively few works have addressed the visual exploration of data provenance. Moreover, for large datasets, provenance annotations can become very large and difficult to interpret manually. Although approaches for provenance summarization and approximation have been proposed, effectively presenting provenance information while preserving its relevant semantics remains an open challenge.
