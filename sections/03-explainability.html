Explaining Data Pipelines

Building on the previous foundations, we present two representative examples. The first is a baseline data lineage scenario, illustrating how individual output tuples produced through common operations such as selection, projection, join, union and aggregation, can be traced back to the source data that contributed to their derivation. The second case introduces a root-cause analysis for an employee promotion prediction model. It puts in evidence how intermediate data outside the training dataset can quietly condition model results, and how provenance polynomials allow to trace the underlying pathways through diverse perspectives, namely the data and the pipeline methods themselves. This provides the means to analyze the upstream data preparation to detect fairness anomalies that favor or prejudice specific employee groups, and isolate their root causes. While algebraic annotations precisely capture data dependencies, they can be hard to interpret in large datasets or complex pipelines. To overcome this, we combine algebraic representations with graphical visualizations, making data derivation paths and their underlying dependencies easier to understand. Importantly, the provenance-based analysis is model agnostic, remaining independent of the predictive model itself.

Data Lineage

This is a simple example to show how to identify the input tuples that contributed to a given output record, establishing a direct link between source and derived data, and providing a lightweight way to trace how data flows across processing pipelines.

The visual representation of data derivations supports an exploratory analysis of data provenance traces. It facilitates the discovery of contribution patterns, dependencies and influential source tuples that may be difficult to identify by inspecting provenance annotations alone. For instance, selecting an output tuple allows users to find the corresponding training actions or programs that contributed to its derivation, as well as the underlying datasets. Conversely, selecting a training action makes it easy to identify which employees performed it. The visual analysis of the data also provides a quick overview of the most popular training programs. Beyond exploratory use, the annotated datasets also provide a reliable basis for machine learning, allowing provenance relationships to be explored throughout model development, evaluation, and explainability.


Fairness and trustworthiness

While lineage identifies which source tuples contributed to a result, provenance polynomials provide a richer explanation of how and why those tuples contributed by capturing the structure of their derivations, including alternative and joint contributions. For aggregate queries, semimodule annotations further associate each contributing tuple with the value it contributes to the aggregate, making it possible to determine not only which tuples influenced the result, but also how their individual values contributed to the final aggregate.

In this example, we present a scenario related to employee performance evaluations and promotions, for the company with two business sectors presented in Example 1. The data structures are as follows:

Unified data

Employees(empId, name, gender, birthdate, department, hiredDate, prov)
Managers(managerId, empId, prov)
Promotions(empId, date, oldPosition, newPosition, decision, decisionSource, comments)

Business sector data

Business_reviews(empId, year, score, prov)
Manager_reviews(empId, year, score, managerId, prov)
The score is a value in 0...100.

Industrial sector data

Annual_reviews(empId, year, officialScore, managerScore, managerId, prov)
The officialScore is also a value in 0...100, but the manager score is a letter in A, B, ..., E.


The objective is to analyze employees who were promoted and those who were not, as determined by a model, algorithm or human decision-makers. The analysis aims to identify potential biases and disparities in promotion decisions, including differences associated with employees’ gender, department or business sector.

The dataset indicates an initial distribution where 83% of the candidates were promoted and 17% were not. By tracing the derivation paths linking promotion outcomes back to the source data, we observed a higher density of paths corresponding to non-promotions that pass through the Annual_reviews table compared to those routed through the join of Business_reviews and Manager_reviews. Building on this insight, a localized analysis reveals that 29% of the employees with evaluations in Annual_reviews resulted in non-promotion, contrasting sharply with a rejection rate of only 9% across the remaining derivation pathways. While the global average across the three performance scores, namely Manager_reviews.score, Business_reviews.score, and Annual_reviews.officialScore (all normalized to a 0–100 scale), remained consistent between 70 and 71/100, the discrepancy was traced to the scaling of managerScores. Specifically, the conversion mapping of letter grades (A–E) to the 100-point numerical scale introduced a systemic downward shift, pulling its specific localized average down to 63/100. These scores are not actually part of the promotions dataset itself, but they have been used to compute the scores that can influence the outcomes.
